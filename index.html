Social Security

When to claim matters

You get a different monthly check depending on when you file. The longer you wait (up to age 70), the bigger the check — and it's inflation-adjusted for life. This is a separate decision from when we stop working.

Dave's Social Security benefit by claim age
Waiting from 62 to 70 nearly doubles the monthly benefit. Stephie's spousal benefit is based on Dave's number.

Dave's options

  • Age 62: $2,192/mo
  • Age 67 (full): $3,269/mo
  • Age 70 (max): $4,085/mo

Stephie's spousal benefit

  • Up to 50% of Dave's full benefit: ~$1,635/mo
  • She can't claim until Dave files
  • She's 2.5 years older, so her timing affects what's optimal
A strategy worth considering

Retire earlier, claim Social Security later. We have enough in the brokerage to "bridge" the gap. Delaying Dave's Social Security to 70 gives us a bigger inflation-protected paycheck for life and leaves more for Stephie if Dave dies first.

The healthcare bridge

Getting from retirement to Medicare

Medicare starts at 65. If we retire earlier, we need private coverage to fill the gap. Good news: there's a legitimate strategy that can cut this cost significantly.

We currently pay $17,892/yr for three people. Once TJ ages off, expect $15-25K/yr for just the two of us on an ACA plan.

The ACA subsidy angle Planning opportunity

ACA premium tax credits are based on taxable income, not net worth. If we retire early and live mostly off brokerage sales (where only the gain portion is taxable), we could show low taxable income and qualify for significant subsidies — potentially cutting healthcare costs to $5-10K/yr. This needs real planning but can be worth tens of thousands over the 62-to-65 window.

What we should do now

Action items, in order

These are the practical moves that materially improve the plan. Most are decisions, not huge lifts.

01

Talk to the CPA about Dave's W-2 salary

At $80K, the Solo 401(k) max is about $62K/yr — not enough to hit our contribution targets. Raising the W-2 to $140-150K roughly doubles the tax-advantaged savings capacity. Tradeoff is more payroll tax, but the retirement math wins. Needs to meet "reasonable compensation" rules for an S-corp.

02

Set up a Solo 401(k), not a SEP

A Solo 401(k) allows Roth employee contributions; a SEP does not. Given our complete lack of Roth money today, the Roth option is worth a lot. Fidelity and Schwab both offer these for free.

03

Put Stephie on the S-corp payroll

If she does any real work for the business, even part-time, paying her a W-2 opens her own Solo 401(k) ($31K Roth potential), builds her own Social Security record, and adds another 25% employer match. Could be worth $40-50K/yr in additional retirement savings.

04

Get cost basis and allocation numbers on the brokerage

This is the biggest unknown. We need to know (a) how much of the $2.125M is gains vs. original investment, and (b) how it's split between stocks, bonds, and cash. Log into the brokerage and look for "unrealized gains" and the allocation summary. Takes 10 minutes and it materially shapes the tax strategy.

05

Start a Roth conversion plan for post-retirement years

Between retiring and age 75 (when required withdrawals start), we'll likely have several years of low taxable income. That's prime time to convert traditional IRA dollars to Roth at low tax rates. This isn't urgent but belongs on the radar.

06

Decide on the mortgage

$200K left in Leesburg. If the rate is under 5%, keep it — the portfolio earns more. If higher, consider paying it off at retirement. Either way it's a rounding error on the overall plan.

07

Revisit long-term care

We're at the right age to consider LTC insurance or a hybrid life/LTC policy. Self-insuring is also legitimate at our asset level. Worth one conversation with an independent broker, not urgent.

Honest uncertainty

Where these numbers could be off

This plan uses reasonable middle-of-the-road assumptions. Here's what I'm less sure about, so we go in with eyes open.

  • Investment returns. I assumed 6% real return pre-retirement, 5% in retirement. Historically reasonable, but a bad decade is possible. Our low withdrawal rate gives us a big cushion.
  • Social Security estimates. The SSA figures assume Dave keeps earning at current levels until retirement. If Dave retires at 62 and stops contributing earlier, the actual benefit could be 5-10% lower.
  • Stephie's benefit. Modeled as spousal only. If she goes on payroll or has earnings history I don't know about, her own benefit could beat the spousal amount.
  • Healthcare costs. Highly variable. ACA rules could change. The $5-25K/yr range is based on today's market.
  • Tax law. Income tax brackets and capital gains rates will change over 15-20 years. We plan with today's rules and adjust.
  • Inflation. I used 2.5% and showed numbers in today's dollars. Higher inflation hurts bonds and cash, but our portfolio is equity-heavy enough to mostly keep up.
  • The "no clue" items. Cost basis, exact asset allocation, and concentration risk in the brokerage are real unknowns that could shift the tax strategy meaningfully once we have them.
